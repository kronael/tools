# Audit: research-onepager-copy.md

Audited file: `research-onepager-copy.md` in this directory.
Compared against `~/.claude/skills/readme/onepager.md`, `~/.claude/skills/writing/SKILL.md`, `~/.claude/skills/writing/page.md`, `~/.claude/skills/readme/sync.md` § Rules and `~/.claude/CLAUDE.md` § Documentation.

A rule is named by section and bullet: R5.2 is the second bullet of section 5. "Order 4" is item 4 of the proposed section order.

Sources were re-fetched on 2026-11-11. Not re-fetched: digital.gov, CXL, the Schwartz summary, the Chronicle piece, ESMA, the FTC dark-patterns report, daytradingtoolkit, financemagnates. The research marks most of these WEAK itself.

## Eight citations do not say what the rule says

1. R5.2 inverts its source. The FCA page lists "Requirements not to diminish information mean you can't use certain words like 'but'" in its column of misunderstandings. The research states it as the rule.
2. R8.1 reports a test that does not exist. The Stripe Atlas guide suggests "Get the Sampler" as a rewrite of "Try Now". It reports no comparison, and it cites no data for any of its advice. "Beat" is invented.
3. R1.5 turns a narrow rule into "avoid hedges". GOV.UK says "Avoid more complicated terms like 'You may be able to'" under "Make requirements clear", for an optional action where "can" is exact. It says nothing about uncertain outcomes.
4. R6.5 and R6.6 quote text that neither cited NN/g article contains. No truncated axis, no "emphasize a finding", no "manipulating a chart", no audience summary test. Only "People need to compare a number against another number in order to interpret it" is there.
5. R1.12 misstates the number and has no evidence behind it. The Home Office page says "a maximum reading age of 9", not a target near 9, and cites no research. The DfE post says it removed reading age from its standard and names no figure.
6. R4.1 reverses Hopkins. He says a specific claim is believed because "People do not expect an advertiser to lie" and "a definite statement is usually accepted". That describes why specificity persuades whether or not it is true. The substantiation duty comes from the SEC and FTC texts in section 5, not from him.
7. R4.2 has no source for "reason why" or for placing it beside the claim. The cited Hopkins text does not use the term. Its lamp example says a precise figure makes people "realize that you have made tests", which is a credibility effect.
8. R4.6 has no source for its test. deceptive.design does not mention Cialdini and does not say a true cue is acceptable. The research admits the gap and keeps the rule.

Smaller defects:

- R5.7 cites Q14 for missing data on typical outcomes. Q14 covers missing expense data. Q23 covers missing earnings data.
- R1.11 cites the 1997 proposing release, Release 33-7380, as the rule. "25-30 words" is commentary in that release, not rule text. Rule 421(d) covers the cover page, summary and risk factors of a prospectus.
- R2.2 rests on a desktop study at 1920×1080. The article does not mention mobile. A link from a friend opens on a phone. The sentence "So 'why is this possible' must sit within the first three screens" does not follow from a viewing-time average.
- R1.1 rests on one 1997 test of one tourism text in five versions. The article gives no participant count. "Measured usability" there is task performance on finding facts, not trust or decision quality.
- R5.10 rests on a 2016 qualitative study of Singapore consumer-service sites with no stated sample size.
- R7.1 cites a URL that returned 404 on fetch. The model it describes is for teacher-led science lessons.
- R6.1 is supported by the abstract for health statistics. The research itself notes that the effect is debated.

Section 5 borrows the FCA's lenient regime and omits the strict one. The cited page covers "mainstream" products only and excludes high-risk investments. For those the FCA banned "certain incentives to invest, such as 'refer a friend bonuses'" and requires "clearer and more prominent risk warnings". Neither regime binds this page. A document about a referral page, for a product that R7.3 says most entrants lose the fee on, picks the lenient one and leaves the referral ban out.

The research also stops short in two sources it cites. FINRA 2210(d)(1)(F) begins "Communications may not predict or project performance". FTC MLM Q16 says a hypothetical earnings scenario "may imply that the assumptions made ... are consistent with the actual experiences of typical participants". Both bear on a worked example and on an illustration chart. Neither is mentioned.

The opening of section 5 states a legal conclusion with no authority: "probably not regulated as a financial promotion", "covered by FTC earnings-claim law". The research names no jurisdiction for the operator or the reader. That sentence must not reach a skill file.

Verified as quoted: the NN/g figures 79%, 58%, 47%, 27%, 124%, 57%, 74% and 81%, the F-pattern recommendations, GOV.UK on 25 words, 5 sentences, "buy" and negative contractions, the SEC handbook on defined terms and the Mr. Smith example, the Rule 421(b) note, the FTC MLM Q13 quotes, the FTC 2026 blog, FINRA 2210(d)(1)(B) to (D), the SEC Marketing Rule fact sheet, the substance of the Endorsement Guides Q&A, the Gigerenzer abstract, the Hopkins quotes in chapters 3 and 7.

## Nine rules produce bad copy when followed to the letter

1. R1.5, no hedges. "You may get paid" becomes "you get paid". That is the "promissory" claim FINRA 2210(d)(1)(B) names, and it deletes the stated uncertainty that `SKILL.md` says to keep.
2. R1.4, present tense. A past result written in the present tense reads as what the reader will get. FINRA 2210(d)(1)(F) bars implying that past performance will recur.
3. R3.3, prefer a figure. Hopkins's test is that a precise number sounds tested. Followed literally the rule puts the largest precise percentage in the headline. The owner already rejected "+478%" up front.
4. R6.1 and Order 5, "x out of 100". With a handful of accounts on record, scaling to 100 invents a sample. The honest form is the real count.
5. R6.3, expected net. An expected value needs a probability the record does not hold, so the rule makes the writer invent one. A mean also hides the outcome that matters to a reader for whom a few thousand dollars matter: losing every fee.
6. R1.9 and Order 3, one worked person. A made-up person with made-up amounts is a hypothetical earnings claim under FTC MLM Q16. The rule does not say the case must come from recorded results.
7. R3.5 and R8.3, prove the claim "without any cost or risk". Hopkins means a product sample. Copied onto an offer whose fee is at risk, it yields "risk-free" wording.
8. R4.8, "unless true and dated". The rule licenses a true countdown or a true "spots left" line. A true pressure cue is still pressure.
9. R2.6 and Order 7, "[claim] even if [objection]". The formula answers every doubt with yes. "Is this a scam?" answered by formula is a sales line, and the reader hears it as one.

## Seven pairs of rules contradict each other

1. R2.6 wants "claim even if objection". R5.2 wants no wording that diminishes a warning. The formula exists to diminish the objection.
2. R1.12 wants reading age 9. R6.3 wants cost, probability, payout and expected net.
3. R8.5 wants the call to action above the fold and repeated. R2.4 wants the mechanism taught before the offer. The proposed order has one next step, last.
4. R2.2 says 81% of viewing time falls in three screens. The proposed order puts typical outcomes fifth and losses sixth of nine.
5. R1.10 says avoid repeated disclosure. R2.7 restates the offer and results at the close. Section 9 restates sections 3 to 5.
6. Order 8 wants attributed testimonials. R5.10 reports that readers distrust on-site testimonials.
7. R1.1 says objective language raises measured usability. R5.9 says marketing must be identifiable as marketing. A pitch in a neutral register gains credit that the second rule exists to withhold.

R3.6 puts the loss in the opening block and R5.3 says risks need not come first. These two agree once the loss shares the block with the gain. The research never says so.

## The borrowed rules assume a setting this page does not have

- Regulated finance. COBS binds FCA-authorised firms, FINRA 2210 binds member firms, the SEC rule binds investment advisers. None binds this page. The research mixes US, UK and EU sources and states no jurisdiction. Only the principles carry over: balance, net of cost, no selected subset, no projection, qualifier beside the claim. The mechanics do not: prescribed periods, compliance review, standard warnings.
- MLM. FTC Q13 asks for the typical participant's result. That presumes a population with income data. A private page has a few accounts. The form that carries over is every result with its count.
- Endorsement Guides. The duty attaches to the endorser's message and the brand. A static page cannot know who sent it, so Order 2 "Who sent this" cannot be written. The page can state a referral reward if the record shows one. The project memory names none, so "what they earn" is unverified. The Guides' own Q&A says a private reply to someone who already knows the affiliation "probably wouldn't need a disclosure".
- Ad copy. Hopkins, Schwartz, Wiebe and CXL tune cold traffic for conversion. Here the reader arrives trusting a friend, so the strongest persuasion is already off the page. Conversion devices add pressure where the reader's guard is down. The research treats liking as one Cialdini tool among six and gives no rule for it.
- Web usability. NN/g scroll and trust findings come from desktop sessions on commercial sites. `onepager.md` caps the page near 500 words, so there is little page to scroll.
- Government plain language. GOV.UK and the Home Office write obligations for the whole population. The stated reader knows some trading and games.
- Health statistics. Gigerenzer's natural frequencies fix errors with conditional probabilities in screening. A single pass rate has no conditional. What carries over is the named reference class and absolute amounts.
- Teaching analogies. The model assumes a teacher who checks understanding. A page gets one read.
- The reader model. The research has the reader buy and trade a challenge himself and cites industry pass rates of 5 to 14%. The project memory describes the reader funding fees while the operator runs the accounts. Industry pass rates for self-directed traders are then the wrong reference class in either direction.

Sources: [FTC Endorsement Guides Q&A](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking), [Gigerenzer et al. 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26161749/).

## Eight rules to fold in

Each rule changes what an agent writes on a page that asks the reader for money. B1 to B7 fit as one short block in `onepager.md`. B8 fits the link rule in `SKILL.md`. Every rule applies to copy the agent writes. Owner copy stays verbatim, and a clash with it is reported once.

- B1. Put what the reader pays and can lose in the same block as what he gets, the first block included. NEVER only in a footnote, footer or disclosures section. Sources: COBS 4.5.2R(2) on the FCA page, FINRA 2210(d)(1)(C) and (D), FTC MLM Q13 "clear, prominent, and unavoidable". Collides with the recorded propyield placement.
- B2. NEVER show a payout or return without every cost the reader bears subtracted in the same figure. Sources: FTC MLM Q13 "must be subtracted from any revenue earned", SEC Marketing Rule fact sheet on gross and net.
- B3. Show every recorded result with its count, or the typical one. NEVER the best result, a chosen period or a hypothetical case as the example. A "results not typical" line does not repair it. Sources: FTC MLM Q13 and Q16, SEC Marketing Rule fact sheet on extracted performance and time periods, FTC blog of 16 June 2026.
- B4. State a loss as an amount and its condition in the offer's own terms. NEVER a generic warning line. Source: FCA page, behavioural testing showing that consumers often do not read "capital at risk".
- B5. Write a past result in the past tense with its dates. NEVER word it as what the reader will get, and NEVER project it forward. Source: FINRA 2210(d)(1)(B) and (F).
- B6. Give a rate as a real count over a named group, "3 of 7 accounts", and give money in and money out before any percentage. Source: Gigerenzer et al. 2007, frequency statements over single-event probabilities and absolute over relative figures. Transfer from health statistics is by analogy.
- B7. When the record shows a reward for the person who sends the link, state the reward in the page body. Source: FTC Endorsement Guides Q&A on material connections and on hyperlinked disclosures being "easily avoidable".
- B8. Make a link or button name what the reader gets on the click. NEVER "Learn more" or "Click here". Source: NN/g link labels, https://www.nngroup.com/articles/better-link-labels/. The evidence is qualitative.
